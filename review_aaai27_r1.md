# AAAI-27 Review — Submission: "Use LLMs to Build Repair Tools, Not Patch Generation: Cost-Effective Automated Program Repair" (CoEP)

**Reviewer:** R1 (APR / SE4AI / LLM-systems)

---

## 1. Summary

The paper targets automated repair of buggy student submissions to programming assignments and argues that the dominant deployment paradigm — one LLM query per submission — wastes money because ~79% of each prompt is byte-identical across submissions to the same assignment. The proposed system, CoEP, instead spends the LLM once per assignment: a builder agent reads the specification, tests, reference solutions, and 5 sample buggy submissions, and synthesizes an executable symbolic repairer (problem-specific AST rewrite rules over a generic bounded edit search), refined for at most 3 harness-driven rounds, with every patch independently test-validated and capped at relative patch size (RPS) ≤ 0.35. Deployed submissions are repaired locally at zero marginal LLM cost; failures escalate to conventional per-submission LLM repair. On 5 assignments from the public Refactory corpus (200 held-out submissions of 1,685), the local repairers fix 47.5%; the hybrid reaches 90.0–90.5% "quality repair rate" (test-pass AND RPS ≤ 0.35) versus 86.0% for the same-model per-submission baseline (McNemar p = 0.008), with build cost $0.256/assignment breaking even at ≈154–295 submissions and marginal cost 1.9–2.5x below the frontier baseline. Ablations show the synthesized rules (not the generic search, 6.5%) carry the local path, refinement rescues 3/5 builders, and a cheap-model builder collapses to 10%.

## 2. Strengths and Weaknesses

### Strengths

**S1 — A genuinely useful reframing with a clean, verifiable cost model.** The many-instances/one-specification observation is crisp, the cost anatomy (Table 1) makes it concrete, and the break-even algebra is internally consistent: I verified 0.256/(2.73−1.43)e-3 ≈ 197, 0.256/(2.73−1.07)e-3 ≈ 154, 0.256/(1.94−1.07)e-3 ≈ 295, the $1.28 total build from 51,227 in + 75,015 out tokens at $3/$15, and that the "ten builder calls" figure matches the RQ3 narrative (2 one-shot + 2 + 3 + 3 rounds). This level of auditable cost accounting is rare in APR papers.

**S2 — Same-model paradigm control and paired statistics.** Holding the LLM fixed across baseline and builder, applying one validation harness and one metric to all eight arms, and using exact McNemar tests with Wilson CIs is the right skeleton. The contamination discussion (symmetry argument, plus the observation that memorized text cannot pass the execution harness by itself) is more honest than most papers on this corpus.

**S3 — Informative ablations.** The generic-only floor (6.5%, discordant 0:82, p ≈ 4e-25), the refinement-round analysis with concrete delivery-defect diagnoses (ast.unparse reformatting blowing the size cap), and the Haiku-builder collapse (10.0%) genuinely isolate where the power comes from. The finding that repairer synthesis is a frontier-capability, delivery-dominated task is a real contribution beyond the headline numbers.

**S4 — Safety-by-abstention design fits the education setting.** The repairer can only abstain, never emit an unvalidated patch; 0 test-failing and 0 over-cap patches in 200 attempts. Auditability of a frozen .py artifact is a legitimate pedagogical advantage over opaque per-query generation.

**S5 — Honest limitation reporting in places.** The paper itself flags top_k (108 submissions) as below break-even, reports per-assignment local heterogeneity (25–85%), and releases raw strings for re-tokenization.

### Weaknesses

**W1 — The headline quality win is manufactured by asymmetric enforcement, and the missing baseline is exactly the one the claim needs.** The primary metric is test-pass AND RPS ≤ 0.35. CoEP enforces the cap with a validator and unlimited local retry; every per-submission LLM arm is restricted by fiat to a single sample with no validation or retry ("single-sample (n=1), matching the deployment setting"). But nothing about the per-submission paradigm forbids validation: PyDex — the system the "PyDex-style" baseline is named after — actually generates many candidates, validates them against the tests, and ranks by token edit distance; ChatRepair iterates on feedback. A per-submission arm with the same enforcement budget (best-of-k under the cap, or a single retry on cap violation, est. cost 2.73 + 0.14 × 2.73 ≈ 3.1 m$ — still far below the few-shot arm's 6.53) would very plausibly close the 4–4.5pp gap, since the baseline already test-passes 100% and busts the cap on only 14%. The paper's own results demonstrate the mechanism: adding a cheap first attempt to the fallback (CoEP+cascade, effectively a second sample plus filter) raises quality 90.0 → 90.5. "A prompt can request minimality, but only a validator can enforce it" is true — but the validator is a few lines of local code available to both paradigms, and only one side gets it. This is the objection most likely to sink the paper in discussion: with enforcement parity, the defensible claim reduces to cost, not quality.

**W2 — Multiple headline claims are contradicted by the paper's own reported numbers.** Concretely:
- (a) "Smallest patches of any arm" (RQ1 box; repeated in Discussion as "CoEP's patches are the smallest per arm"): Table 1's mean-RPS column, computed "over each arm's cap-compliant repairs," shows CoEP-local at 0.127 versus frontier 0.102, PyDex-style 0.108, generic-only 0.023. The text supports the claim only by comparing CoEP's cap-compliant mean (0.127) against the frontier's mean over all test-passing patches (0.187) — an apples-to-oranges comparison.
- (b) "The majority of submissions never reach a model" (Related Work) and "most submissions never touch a model" (Fig. 1 caption): the local rate is 47.5%, i.e., a minority; 52.5% of traffic still requires a model round trip.
- (c) RQ1 box: the hybrid "more than doubles the non-LLM tool's 56.0%" — 90.5/56.0 = 1.62x, not more than double.
- (d) Abstract: "runs in milliseconds" vs. measured mean 0.9 s/submission (Table 1), and an internal 0.8 s vs. 0.9 s inconsistency between the Deployment paragraph and Table 1/RQ1 text.
- (e) Contribution bullet claims "exact token-level cost accounting," but the Protocol admits a chars/3.6 tokenizer approximation because "provider tokenizers are unavailable offline" — implausible given that every API response reports exact usage token counts; either the accounting is exact or it is not.
- (f) Table 1's component means (241 + 92 + 99 = 432) do not sum to the stated 439 total, and the quoted shares (56/21/23) are computed over 432.
None of these is individually fatal, but together they indicate systematic over-statement, and (a)–(c) appear in boxed takeaways and the architecture figure.

**W3 — Statistical support is thin exactly where the claims are strongest.** The only significance test backing the paradigm comparison (p = 0.008, discordant 0:8) is for the frontier-fallback configuration (90.0), yet the abstract/conclusion headline pairs the untested cascade configuration (90.5) against 86.0. The "dominant configuration" claim (90.5 vs. cascade-alone 88.5) rests on 4 of 200 submissions with no test and overlapping CIs. Moreover, if the hybrid's fallback reuses the baseline's responses (the paper never says), the 0:8 discordance and "never does worse" are true by construction, and the McNemar test verifies an identity rather than an empirical result. All LLM arms are single-sample with no run-to-run variance estimate, n = 200 is an 12% subsample of the available 1,685 (Refactory's original evaluation used the full corpus; the AAAI-25 assignment-repair papers evaluate on thousands of programs), per-assignment analyses rest on n = 40, and no multiplicity control is applied across the many pairwise comparisons. The claimed "±5pp Wilson intervals" are actually ±6.9pp at 47.5%.

**W4 — The cost-structure claim proves less than advertised, by the paper's own critique.** The abstract dismisses caching because it "shrink[s] only the constant, not the structure." But with 47.5% local coverage, CoEP's hybrid also leaves cost linear in enrollment — 52.5% of submissions still pay a per-query frontier/cascade round trip — so the achieved improvement is a 1.9–2.5x constant-factor reduction plus an intercept, not a structural change (Fig. 3's "only amortization moves cost into a one-off intercept" is accurate only for the 47.5% slice). The regime where the paradigm claim would be real (high local coverage) is exactly where the evidence is weakest: 25% local on remove_extras, and the whole evaluation covers 5 tiny CS1 functions (5–11 tests) from one course, one language, one model family. The premise also requires assignments to recur unchanged across offerings; instructors rotate assignments (not least for plagiarism), and repairer staleness under spec/test drift is unaddressed.

**W5 — Baseline handicaps and an unexplained external anomaly.** (i) The Refactory tool scores 90.5% test-pass here, well below the ~97–98% its authors report on the same corpus in the same strongest configuration; the paper attributes nothing to the Python 3.11 port and re-parsed tests, but a 7pp drop in a comparison arm needs reconciliation before "more than doubles [56.0%]"-style contrasts are meaningful (the 56.0% quality figure additionally depends on the paper's own τ). (ii) The caching baseline is handicapped by the paper's own prompt ordering: placing task instructions (23% of input) after the student code caps the cacheable prefix at 55%; reordering — trivial and standard — makes 77% cacheable and cuts per-query cost to ≈1.81 m$ (a ~34% saving vs. the reported 23%), shifting the cached break-even beyond the reported 257. (iii) Contamination is handled by argument alone; one clean, post-cutoff assignment would have cost little and settled it.

**W6 — Novelty is a combination, and thinner than the framing suggests.** Per-assignment amortization is precisely CLARA/Refactory's move (acknowledged); LLM-synthesizes-a-reusable-tool is Voyager/LATM (acknowledged); generate–validate–refine is standard agentic practice by 2026. The genuinely new elements are the deployment-economics formulation and the careful cost instrumentation — valuable, but the technical delta is modest against the AAAI-25 assignment-repair bar (counterexample-guided and minimal-modification repair), which brought new algorithms evaluated at substantially larger scale.

## 3. Questions for the Authors

1. Were the hybrid's fallback LLM calls fresh queries, or were the baseline arm's responses reused? If reused, what exactly does the McNemar test (discordant 0:8) establish beyond the construction "local wins ∪ baseline ≥ baseline"?
2. Enforcement parity: report a per-submission frontier arm with the same validator budget CoEP enjoys — best-of-k sampling under the RPS cap, or one retry on cap violation (PyDex's actual multi-candidate, edit-distance-ranked protocol). What are its quality repair rate and cost? This is the comparison the central claim requires.
3. Reconcile "smallest patches of any arm" with Table 1's mean-RPS column (CoEP-local 0.127 vs. frontier 0.102, PyDex-style 0.108 on cap-compliant repairs).
4. Reconcile your Refactory reproduction (90.5% test-pass) with the ~97–98% reported by its authors on the same dataset and configuration. What did the Python 3.11 port and test re-parsing change?
5. Why chars/3.6 rather than the provider-reported exact usage tokens returned with every API response? Will you replace the approximation with exact counts, and does the contribution bullet's "exact token-level cost accounting" survive?
6. Provide the τ sweep behind "sweeping it moves absolute rates but not orderings": at what τ does the frontier baseline overtake the hybrid, and is 90.5 vs. 88.5 (cascade) significant under any τ?
7. "The majority of submissions never reach a model" vs. a 47.5% local rate — which is correct, and will the Fig. 1 caption, Related Work, and abstract ("milliseconds") be corrected?
8. Which four of five assignments clear the 154 and 295 break-evens (per-assignment submission counts), and what happens to a frozen repairer when the instructor edits the spec or tests the following term — is the $0.256 build re-paid, and is that included in the recurring-cost story?
9. Have you run the builder on any assignment provably outside the model's training data (e.g., authored post-cutoff)? Even one such data point would substantially strengthen the contamination argument.

## 4. Significance

**Score: 2/4.** The deployment-economics observation is real, practically motivated, and likely to be cited; the builder-capability and delivery-defect findings (RQ3) are of independent interest. But the demonstrated effect is a ~2x constant-factor cost reduction with sub-half local coverage on five CS1-scale functions from one 2019 course, the quality advantage is contingent on an enforcement asymmetry (W1), and generalization to larger assignments, other courses, languages, or model families is untested. As evidenced, this is a useful engineering result, not yet a paradigm result.

## 5. Justification of the Approach

**Score: 2/4.** The library design (generic edit search + validation + failure diagnosis) and the never-trust-self-reports validation are well motivated, and the austere single-shot refinement loop is justified by cost measurement. However, the load-bearing knobs are unjustified against alternatives: τ = 0.35, k = 5 build bugs, R_max = 3, and n = 1 sampling are all fixed without sweeps or sensitivity data (the τ-robustness claim is asserted, not shown), and the most natural alternative design — keep per-submission repair but add the same cheap local validator and retry — is never instantiated (W1), despite being the alternative the paper's thesis must beat. The decision to deny all per-submission arms any validation is defended only by an appeal to "deployment setting" that contradicts how the cited deployed systems (PyDex, ChatRepair) actually work.

## 6. Quality of the Evaluation

**Score: 2/4.** The infrastructure is above average: real student data, seven baselines across three families, same-model control, one harness, paired exact tests, CIs, cost accounting, and three ablations. But the evaluation does not support the headline claims: the decisive quality comparison is confounded by asymmetric enforcement with the parity baseline absent (W1); several boxed claims conflict with the paper's own tables (W2a–c); the strongest configuration's advantages (90.5 vs. 86.0; 90.5 vs. 88.5) are either untested or rest on 4 discordant submissions (W3); n = 200 of 1,685 with n = 40 per assignment is small for the per-assignment conclusions drawn; the Refactory arm underperforms its published numbers without explanation and the caching arm is handicapped by prompt ordering (W5); and the "exact" cost accounting is a chars/3.6 approximation (W2e).

## 7. Reproducibility

**Score: 3/4.** Strong on paper: scripted seeded imports from a public corpus, released prompts, synthesized repairers, raw model responses for both builder tiers, fixed seeds/timeouts/thresholds, deterministic local search, CPU-only, and a completed checklist. Docked one point because the pivotal components are proprietary July-2026 model snapshots (results will not be reproducible once snapshots are retired; no open-model builder is attempted), token counts are approximated rather than taken from API usage metadata, and figures/break-evens therefore inherit a ±20% calibration band the authors themselves acknowledge.

## 8. Ethical Considerations

No serious concerns. The corpus is public and de-identified; no new human-subject data. The ethical statement appropriately scopes the system to feedback (not grading) under instructor oversight, and the escalation design avoids degraded service for students whose programs the local repairer cannot handle. Two points deserve a sentence in revision: (i) patches are validated only against the visible tests, so students can receive "repairs" that overfit an incomplete suite and are still wrong — instructor-facing disclosure of this failure mode should be required; (ii) the pedagogy-displacement discussion (students receiving auto-fixes rather than learning to debug) is acknowledged but thin.

## 9. Overall Evaluation

**Overall: 5/10** (marginally below the acceptance threshold).

A well-written paper with a genuinely attractive idea, verifiable cost arithmetic, and unusually careful instrumentation — but the central quality claim ("exceeds the per-submission baseline") is manufactured by giving only one paradigm a validator (W1), several boxed claims are contradicted by the paper's own tables (W2), and the statistically supported result is not the configuration headlined (W3). If the authors (a) add an enforcement-parity per-submission baseline and re-state claims accordingly, (b) correct the "smallest patches," "majority," "doubles," "milliseconds," and "exact accounting" statements, and (c) reconcile the Refactory reproduction, this becomes a solid systems/economics contribution — likely a 6–7. As submitted, the claims outrun the evidence.

**Confidence: 4/5.** I know the assignment-repair literature (CLARA, Refactory, Verifix, PyDex) and LLM-cost systems work well, verified the paper's arithmetic directly, and am confident in the identified claim-evidence gaps; I could not re-run the artifacts or inspect the figures.
