# AAAI-27 Review — Submission: "Use LLMs to Build Repair Tools, Not Patch Generation: Cost-Effective Automated Program Repair"

Reviewer: R1 (APR / SE4AI / LLM-systems)

---

## 1. Summary

The paper targets automated repair of buggy student submissions to programming assignments and argues that the prevailing deployment paradigm — one LLM query per submission, re-transmitting the assignment description and tests every time — is economically mismatched with a regime the authors call "many instances, one specification." Their system, CoEP, instead spends the LLM once per assignment: a builder model reads the specification, test suite, 3 reference solutions, and 5 historical buggy submissions, and synthesizes an executable symbolic repairer (problem-specific AST rewrite rules over a provided generic bounded edit-search library), which is refined through at most 3 validation-feedback rounds and then deployed locally; every patch is independently re-validated (test pass plus a relative-patch-size cap of 0.35), and submissions the repairer cannot fix escalate to a conventional per-submission LLM path. On 5 assignments from the public Refactory dataset (1,685 real CS1 Python submissions; 200 held-out for the eight-arm comparison, all LLM arms driven by the same frontier model), the synthesized repairers alone fix 47.5% of held-out submissions (50.7% [48.3, 53.1] on the full corpus) with zero unsafe outputs, and the hybrid preserves each fallback protocol's quality while cutting marginal cost 1.8–1.9x — notably matching an enforced-retry baseline's 98.5% quality on the identical 197/200 submissions at $1.78e-3 vs. $3.26e-3 per submission. Build cost is $0.256 per assignment, with per-assignment break-evens of 38–704 submissions (3 of 5 assignments inside one course offering). Ablations attribute the local power to the synthesized rules (generic search alone: 6.5%) and to frontier-tier builder capability (Haiku builder: 10.0%), with refinement rescuing 3 of 5 builders.

## 2. Strengths and Weaknesses

### Strengths

**S1 — A crisp, well-quantified problem framing that is genuinely new for APR deployment.** The cost-anatomy section is concrete rather than rhetorical: 77% of the 439-token per-submission prompt is byte-identical across a class (I verified 340/439 = 77.4%), only 55% sits in a contiguous cacheable prefix, and the prompt falls below the provider's 1,024-token caching minimum, so the standard "just cache it" rebuttal is empirically foreclosed on this workload. The observation that provider caching serves real redundancy "at the wrong granularity" is a sharp and correct point. Amortizing specification-level reasoning into an executable, auditable per-assignment artifact — rather than amortizing data into a retrieval index (the AAAI-26 learner-tailored line, which still pays per query) — is a distinct and articulable paradigm contribution.

**S2 — Unusually self-adversarial experimental design.** The paper anticipates and instruments nearly every objection a reviewer would raise: same-model control across all LLM arms (paradigm, not model, is the treatment); an information-parity arm (PyDex-style few-shot receives the same assignment-specific budget the builder gets: 85.5% at 2.4x cost); an *enforcement-parity* arm (validator + 1 retry on the baseline, 98.5%) that deliberately dismantles the paper's own most quotable win (see W3) and reframes the claim as cost-at-matched-quality; zero-build-cost competitors (caching bound, Haiku, FrugalGPT cascade, generic-search-only, Refactory-tool-fronted fallback); a retrieval upper bound (patch reuse covers at most 33% vs. rules' 50.7%); a hidden-oracle audit for suite overfitting (92.6% vs. 97.1% probe agreement) *with a deployed mitigation* (probe as third validator: coverage 47.5→44.0%, hybrid quality unchanged); an independent end-to-end replication of the frontier pipeline (43.0% vs. 47.5% local coverage; hybrid 88.0% vs. 90.0%); and a builder-capability ablation. This is well above the evaluation hygiene of typical accepted APR papers at this venue.

**S3 — Headline numbers verify.** I independently recomputed every checkable figure and all reproduce exactly: the McNemar p-values from the stated discordant pairs (0:8 → 0.0078, 7:0 → 0.0156, 0:9 → 0.0039, 4:0 → 0.125 n.s.); all break-evens from Table 2 costs (197, 154, 295, 173, and 229 for the builder upgrade); the base cost $2.73e-3 from 439/94 tokens at $3/$15 per Mtok; the Haiku $0.90e-3; build cost $1.28 total / $0.256 per assignment from the stated token counts; the 23% caching bound; all five Wilson CIs to the decimal ([48.3, 53.1], [40.7, 54.4], [80.5, 90.1], [85.1, 93.4], [3.8, 10.8]); the corpus volumes (sum 1,685, mean 337); and even the adverse-selection accounting — the naive composite costs (0.90 + 0.35×2.73 = $1.86 vs. measured $1.94; 0.525×1.94 = $1.02 vs. measured $1.07) and the implied frontier success rate on Haiku-escalated instances (23.5/35 = 67.1%, stated as 67%). I found no inflated or inconsistent claim.

**S4 — Safety-by-construction with a clean inheritance property.** The design decision that synthesized code is "contractually forbidden" from returning an unvalidated patch, backed by harness-side re-validation, yields an empirically verified abstention-only failure mode (0 test-failing, 0 over-cap patches in 200 attempts) and makes the composition claim exact rather than statistical: CoEP+retry repairs the identical 197/200 submissions as the retry arm (discordant 0:0) at 1.8x lower marginal cost. "Same quality set, smaller slope" is the right claim shape for this kind of paper, and it is demonstrated instance-by-instance rather than on averages.

**S5 — Honest statistics and limitations.** The paper flags that hybrid-vs-baseline McNemar tests are structurally one-sided (the hybrid contains the fallback), reports the cluster-level sign test as not significant (3–0–2 over 5 assignments) and labels the edge "suggestive," discloses the unfavorable per-assignment break-evens (top_k ≈ 704 vs. 108 submissions/offering), reports that the frontier baseline's accepted patches are *smaller* than CoEP's on shared instances (0.093 vs. 0.115 RPS), and confronts contamination head-on with a symmetry argument plus corroborating (if not decisive) evidence. This level of self-report is rare and increases my trust in the unverifiable parts.

**S6 — Strong reproducibility posture.** Raw prompt/response strings for every arm and every builder round (both tiers), synthesized repairers, seeds, deterministic local search, model snapshot identification, and a closed-form break-even that any reader can re-price under their own tokenizer/tier.

### Weaknesses

**W1 — Small testbed; paradigm-level inference rests on n=5 clusters.** Five assignments from one CS1 course, one language, one model family. Submissions cluster within assignments, and at the cluster level the flagship hybrid-vs-plain-baseline edge is not significant (the paper concedes this). The full eight-arm comparison covers only 200 of the 1,685 eligible submissions (40 per assignment); only the zero-cost local arm is run on the full corpus. Given that the frontier baseline costs $2.73e-3 per submission, running the *hybrid* arms on all 1,660 held-out submissions would have cost roughly $5 — the subsampling is defensible for eight arms but leaves an easily-closable gap on the two or three load-bearing ones. Recently accepted AAAI APR papers (e.g., the AAAI-25 counterexample-guided work) evaluate across substantially more assignments, if similarly small programs.

**W2 — The absolute economic stakes on the studied corpus are tiny, and the non-cost benefits are asserted rather than measured.** At $2.73e-3 per submission, repairing every buggy submission in this corpus costs about $4.60 per term; CoEP's total saving at corpus scale is a few dollars per offering. The paper is candid that the claim is a structurally smaller slope (1.8–2.5x) plus an intercept, and the argument gains force for richer protocols (few-shot $6.53e-3, retry $3.26e-3) and MOOC scale — but the practically decisive auxiliary benefits (sub-second local latency vs. API round trips, rate limits at "deadline-night spikes," no external dependence) are claimed without measurement: Table 1 reports sec/sub for the local arms and Refactory but "—" for every LLM arm, and no deadline-spike or throughput experiment exists. For the significance case the paper wants to make, those unmeasured axes matter as much as the dollars.

**W3 — The headline quality win over the "deployed convention" is an enforcement artifact, and the abstract/intro lead with it.** The 90.0% vs. 86.0% (p=0.008) result exists only because the plain baseline lacks a size validator; granting the baseline CoEP's own enforcement (validator + one retry — a trivial, zero-build-cost addition) yields 98.5%, exceeding every CoEP configuration except the one that inherits it. The paper deserves credit for constructing exactly this parity arm and for pivoting the central claim to cost-at-matched-quality, but the abstract's "repairs strictly more within the patch-size cap" and the RQ1 box's "exceeds every deployed single-shot configuration" foreground a comparison against the weakest reasonable deployment. The honest headline is: equal quality at 1.8–1.9x lower marginal cost, plus a modest, cluster-insignificant bonus against unenforced baselines.

**W4 — Contamination threatens the absolute coverage number, which the economics depend on.** The Refactory corpus, including student fixes, has been public since 2019. The symmetry argument protects *paradigm comparisons* (same model in every arm), and the corroborations (refinement rounds needed for ast.unparse formatting defects; the cheap builder's collapse to 10% despite the same putative memorized knowledge) are thoughtful — but neither is decisive, since delivery-level defects are orthogonal to memorized bug-distribution knowledge, and Haiku's collapse may simply reflect weaker code synthesis. The 47.5–50.7% local coverage — the quantity that sets every break-even — could be inflated relative to a genuinely fresh assignment, and no fresh/private assignment probe or paraphrase-perturbation test is attempted.

**W5 — Coverage is modest, heterogeneous, and its drivers are under-explored.** Local repair spans 27.9–71.4% across assignments; two of five assignments do not break even within one offering (sort_age at ~1.2 offerings, top_k at ~6.5). The build-set size k=5 and R_max=3 are fixed with no sensitivity sweep, even though the paper itself notes coverage is "bounded by what five sample bugs can teach" — a k-sweep is the single most obvious missing experiment for a paper whose thesis is an economic trade-off between build investment and coverage.

**W6 — The Refactory-tool comparison mixes metric regimes without granting the tool enforcement parity.** The 34-point "clears the non-LLM tool" claim (90.0/90.5 vs. 56.0) applies this paper's RPS≤0.35 cap to a 2019 system that never optimized for it (its own oracle: 91.0% test-pass, honestly reported within a point of the authors' 90.5%). The LLM baseline is given a validator-plus-retry parity arm; the symbolic tool is not given the analogous courtesy (e.g., post-hoc patch minimization / delta-debugging against the suite), which plausibly recovers a substantial fraction of its 0.386-RPS patches. As it stands, the cross-paradigm quality gap is partly an artifact of who was designed under which metric.

**W7 — Cost accounting is modeled, not billed.** Tokens come from a chars/3.6 approximation (provider tokenizers unavailable offline), one price tier, and the plain-prompt caching row is a synthetic "no-minimum bound" unavailable in practice (disclosed). The release of raw strings and the ±20% sensitivity statement mitigate this, but every dollar figure and break-even inherits that uncertainty, and the paper's core quantitative claims are denominated in those dollars.

**W8 (minor) — Overstatements and terminology.** The abstract's "produces minimal patches by construction" should read "size-capped": on shared instances the baseline's accepted patches are smaller (0.093 vs. 0.115). "Builder agent" is confusing given the protocol's stateless single-shot prompt-response calls. Per-assignment result tables are absent from the main text (ranges in prose only).

## 3. Questions for the Authors

1. In the hybrid arms, are fallback outputs fresh LLM samples or the stored responses from the corresponding per-submission arm? If stored (as the "structurally one-sided" remark implies), please state this explicitly, since it makes "never reversed" true by construction rather than an empirical finding.
2. Can you provide any evidence bounding contamination's effect on the *absolute* local coverage — e.g., synthesis for one unpublished assignment from a current course, or identifier/description-paraphrased variants of the five assignments that break surface memorization while preserving semantics?
3. Why were the hybrid and retry arms not run on the full 1,660-submission corpus (~$5 at your own pricing), given that the local arm was? Would you commit to this for the camera-ready?
4. How does local coverage scale with the number of build bugs k (e.g., k = 1, 10, 20) and with R_max? Where does top_k's 27.9% coverage plateau if the builder sees more of its history?
5. For enforcement parity with Refactory: if you post-minimize the tool's test-passing patches (greedy hunk subtraction / delta debugging against T), how much of the 90.5→56.0 quality drop recovers, and how does Refactory-then-frontier then compare to CoEP+cascade?
6. What are the 3/200 failures of the retry arm — are they bugs that *cannot* be fixed under RPS ≤ 0.35 (i.e., is 98.5% a metric-imposed ceiling), and does CoEP+retry fail on the same instances for the same reason?
7. What is the measured wall-clock latency (median/p95) of the per-submission LLM arms, to substantiate the deadline-spike argument against the local path's 0.8s?
8. Exactly which model snapshots (IDs) were used for builder, baseline, and Haiku arms, and are the released raw strings sufficient to recompute every table without any model access?

## 4. Significance

**Score: 3/4.** The many-instances-one-specification framing is real, previously unnamed in the LLM-APR literature, and the "amortize reasoning into a verifiable executable artifact" recipe plausibly transfers to hints, rubrics, and grading feedback; the head-to-head economics against caching, cascades, retrieval reuse, and a classical symbolic tool constitute a useful map for practitioners. Significance is capped by the niche (CS1 Python, five famous assignments), the small absolute dollar stakes on the studied corpus (W2), and local coverage of only ~half of submissions, which makes the paradigm shift partial rather than decisive.

## 5. Justification of the Approach

**Score: 3/4.** The design is well-motivated and internally coherent: the abstention-only contract plus independent re-validation gives a sound never-worse hybrid; the operator library grounds synthesis; the refinement loop is justified empirically (3/5 builders shipped broken without it); and RQ3 cleanly attributes power to the synthesized rules (6.5% generic floor, discordant 0:82). Held back from 4 by unswept design constants (k=5, R_max=3), by the asymmetric enforcement courtesy extended to the LLM baseline but not the symbolic tool (W6), and by the reliance on a fixed τ=0.35 as the primary quality construct (the τ-sweep helps, but the local arm's numbers below τ=0.35 are conservative recomputations, not re-searches).

## 6. Quality of the Evaluation

**Score: 3/4.** Within its scale this is exemplary: same-model control, eight baselines including information- and enforcement-parity arms, uniform validation and cost accounting, exact paired tests with cluster-level caveats, a hidden-oracle overfitting audit with a deployed fix, a replication run, and verified internal consistency of every reported number (S3). It falls short of 4 on breadth: five assignments from one course, 200-instance arm comparisons where full-corpus runs were cheap (W1), no fresh-assignment contamination probe (W4), no k/R_max sensitivity (W5), and missing latency measurements for the LLM arms (W2).

## 7. Reproducibility

**Score: 3/4.** The checklist is thorough and specific: seeded scripted dataset import, released prompts, per-round builder transcripts for both tiers, synthesized repairers, raw response strings enabling exact re-tokenization and re-pricing, deterministic CPU-only local search, and stated snapshots/timeouts/thresholds. Two things keep it from 4: the core synthesis step is stochastic against proprietary July-2026 snapshots (bit-exact regeneration will be impossible once they retire; the released transcripts make results auditable but not regenerable), and the artifact is promised as supplementary material that I cannot inspect, with dollar figures resting on an approximate tokenizer rather than billed counts.

## 8. Ethical Considerations

No significant concerns. The data are public, previously released, and de-identified student submissions used within their established research purpose; no new human-subject data are collected. The system automates formative feedback, not grading, with instructor oversight and full-LLM treatment for unhandled students, and the repairer artifact is auditable — arguably an ethics improvement over opaque per-query generation. Two points worth a sentence in the camera-ready: (i) pedagogical risk that near-instant automated "fixes" short-circuit productive struggle (the RPS cap mitigates wholesale rewriting but does not address feedback timing/disclosure policy); (ii) equity of the escalation split — students whose bugs the local repairer covers get sub-second deterministic feedback while others depend on API availability, which is the inverse of the usual concern but still a differential experience.

## 9. Overall Evaluation

**Overall: 7/10 (accept).**

This is a systems-and-economics paper rather than an algorithmic one, and it should be judged on the novelty of its framing and the credibility of its measurements. On both it performs well: the per-assignment amortization paradigm is a genuinely new deployment claim for LLM-based assignment repair relative to the per-submission AAAI-25/26 line, and the evaluation is more self-adversarial than most accepted APR papers at this venue — the authors build the baseline that beats their own headline (98.5% retry), then show their system inherits it exactly at 1.8x lower cost, which is the honest and durable version of the claim. Every number I could recompute checks out precisely. The reasons this is a 7 and not an 8: the five-assignment, one-course testbed with cluster-insignificant flagship comparisons; the unresolved contamination threat to the absolute coverage number that all break-evens depend on; the modest and heterogeneous local coverage with no build-set sensitivity analysis; the enforcement-asymmetric treatment of the symbolic tool; and abstract-level framing that leads with the weakest-baseline win. None of these is disqualifying, several are fixable in a camera-ready (Q2–Q5 especially), and the contribution will be useful to both the SE4AI and education-technology communities.

**Confidence: 4/5.** I know the assignment-repair and LLM-APR literature well, verified all checkable arithmetic against the tables and macros, and am confident in the internal-consistency assessment; I could not inspect the supplementary artifact, the figures' rendered content, or the AAAI-26 cited system's exact evaluation scale.
