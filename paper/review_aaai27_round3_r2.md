# AAAI-27 Review — "Use LLMs to Build Repair Tools, Not Patch Generation: Cost-Effective Automated Program Repair"

Reviewer 2 (ML systems / cost-efficient inference)

---

## 1. Summary

The paper observes that LLM-based repair of programming-assignment submissions is deployed one query per submission, although the setting is "many instances, one specification": on the studied corpus, roughly 79% of each repair prompt is byte-identical across all submissions to the same assignment. The proposed system, CoEP, instead spends the LLM once per assignment: a builder agent reads the handout, test suite, 3 reference solutions, and 5 historical buggy submissions, and synthesizes an executable symbolic repairer (problem-specific AST rewrite rules over a generic bounded edit search) that is refined for up to 3 validation-feedback rounds, then deployed locally; submissions it cannot fix escalate to conventional per-submission LLM repair. On 5 assignments and 200 held-out real student submissions from the public Refactory dataset, with the same frontier model powering all LLM arms, the local repairers fix 47.5% at zero marginal LLM cost with zero unsafe outputs, and the hybrid reaches 90.0-90.5% quality repairs (test-pass with a relative-patch-size cap of 0.35) versus 86.0% for the plain per-submission baseline. The cost analysis prices all arms under one token convention: build cost is $0.256 per assignment, break-even is roughly 154-295 submissions, and fronting any per-submission protocol (including an enforced-retry protocol that itself reaches 98.5%) preserves that protocol's quality at a 1.8-2.5x smaller marginal-cost slope. Eight baselines (plain, few-shot, cached, retry, cheap model, cascade, generic-search-only, and the non-LLM Refactory tool) and three ablations (rules vs. generic search, refinement rounds, builder capability) round out the evaluation.

## 2. Strengths and Weaknesses

### Strengths

**S1. The numbers are internally consistent to an unusual degree — I verified them.** I recomputed every checkable quantity. All eight Wilson intervals in Table 1 are exactly correct (e.g., 95/200 -> [40.7, 54.4]; 181/200 -> [85.6, 93.8]). All exact McNemar p-values match their discordant counts (0:8 -> 0.0078; 0:9 -> 0.0039; 4:0 -> 0.125 reported honestly as n.s.; 0:82 -> 4.1e-25, consistent with the stated p<1e-24). The cost arithmetic is coherent end to end: 439 in / 94 out at $3/$15 per Mtok gives 2.727e-3 = the reported 2.73 m$; the cached bound 2.10 is reproducible only if one correctly amortizes the 1.25x cache write over the 40-submission arm, which the authors evidently did; build cost $1.28 = 51,227x$3 + 75,015x$15 per Mtok, i.e., $0.256/assignment = ~94 baseline repairs; the break-evens 197, 154, 295, 173 and the Haiku-builder upgrade break-even (~230) all follow from C/Delta with the table's numbers. Even the discordant counts interlock across sections: CoEP-local fixes 95, the baseline is within-cap on 87 of them ("the 87 instances both repair"), and the remaining 8 are exactly the 0:8 discordant pairs behind the headline p=0.008. This level of cross-checkable coherence is rare and raises my confidence that the pipeline actually ran as described.

**S2. Same-model, same-harness design isolates the paradigm.** The builder and every per-submission arm use the same frontier model, all patches from all arms are re-validated by one harness under one metric, and cost accounting uses one convention. This is the right control for a deployment-paradigm claim, and it also makes the training-data-contamination argument (symmetric inflation) at least coherent.

**S3. The paper includes the baseline that hurts it most, and reports it prominently.** The enforcement-parity arm (validator + one minimality-feedback retry) reaches 98.5% — well above the CoEP+frontier hybrid's 90.0% — and the paper puts it in the abstract-level narrative rather than burying it. The response is the composition result: CoEP+retry repairs the identical 197/200 set (discordant 0:0, per-assignment identical) at 1.78 vs. 3.26 m$/submission. "Same quality set, smaller slope, verified instance-by-instance" is exactly the kind of claim a cost-efficiency paper should make and almost never does.

**S4. Honest cost framing.** The paper explicitly disclaims sublinearity ("hybrid cost is still linear in enrollment---the claim is a 1.8-2.5x smaller slope plus a one-off intercept"), states the break-even formula, reports the low-traffic boundary case (top_k, 108 submissions) as the regime's honest limit, and reports raw token counts so any price sheet substitutes into the closed form.

**S5. The caching analysis is more generous to the competitor than it needs to be.** The claim that the 439-token prompt sits below Anthropic's 1,024-token cacheable minimum is factually correct, and the pricing constants (write 1.25x, read 0.1x, $3/$15 Sonnet, $1/$5 Haiku) match the provider's published schedule. My first instinct was that a deployer would simply pad or restructure the prefix past 1,024 tokens; I computed that counterfactual (~2.39 m$/submission after amortized writes) and it is *worse* than the no-minimum bound of 2.10 the paper already grants caching in Table 2. Since CoEP's configurations (1.07-1.43) beat the over-generous bound, the argument is robust to the obvious rebuttal.

**S6. Ablations target the real questions and include a negative result.** Generic-search-only collapses to 6.5% (so the LLM-synthesized rules, not the search substrate, carry the method — 82/95 = 86% of local fixes); refinement rounds are shown to fix *delivery* defects (ast.unparse reformatting blowing the size cap) with build-set trajectories 15/25 -> 25/25; and the Haiku-tier builder collapse to 10.0% is reported in detail rather than hidden, with a sensible economic interpretation (builder capability is bought once per assignment).

**S7. Safety asymmetry is a real finding, cleanly established.** The local path's zero test-failing and zero over-cap emissions across 200 attempts (abstention as the only failure mode) is what makes the "fronting preserves any fallback's quality set" argument structural rather than empirical luck, and the paper draws exactly that inference and then verifies it empirically for the retry fallback.

### Weaknesses

**W1. The entire quality differentiation hangs on a single untested threshold (tau = 0.35).** Test-pass is saturated at 100% for every LLM arm (Table 1), so *all* quality separation — the 86.0% baseline, the 14% cap-busting rate, Refactory's fall to 56.0%, and the headline "hybrid exceeds the baseline" — flows exclusively through the RPS <= 0.35 cutoff. No sensitivity analysis is provided. At tau = 0.5 the baseline's quality rate would rise (its accepted patches average 0.102, and the over-cap tail is unreported) and the RQ1 superiority claim could shrink or vanish; at tau = 0.25 the local rate would drop too. The cost claims (RQ2) survive any tau, but the effectiveness claims (RQ1) are conditional on one constant justified only by citation lineage ("follows Refactory and PyDex" — which used RPS as a reported metric, not an acceptance cap). A quality-vs-tau curve for the main arms is a one-figure fix and its absence is the paper's largest evaluation gap.

**W2. The headline significance test is structurally one-sided, and the per-assignment "robustness check" inherits the same defect.** The paper discloses (Internal validity) that the hybrid contains the baseline as its fallback, so hybrid-vs-baseline McNemar tests cannot go the other way. Good. But the RQ1 box still advertises "p=0.008, direction consistent across all five assignments" as corroboration: given the superset structure, per-assignment direction *cannot* be negative, so "consistent direction" carries no evidential weight — and one assignment is +0, making "holds in all five" an overstatement even descriptively. The honest headline quantity is "+8/200 quality repairs (4.0 pp), all attributable to cap-enforcement on locally-repaired instances," with a CI, not a hypothesis test whose rejection region is structurally half-empty. The genuinely symmetric evidence the paper offers (CoEP+retry identity; patch sizes on shared instances, where the baseline is actually slightly *better*, 0.093 vs. 0.115) is the right evidence; the abstract and RQ1 box lean on the wrong one.

**W3. Five assignments, one course, one model family; and cluster structure limits what pooled n=200 can claim.** All pooled tests treat submissions as exchangeable, but they cluster within 5 assignments with local rates spanning 25-85%. The paper acknowledges this and reports per-assignment directions, but with 5 clusters the paradigm-level generalization ("assignment repair is amortizable") rests on n=5 in the relevant unit of inference. Relatedly, the "±5pp Wilson intervals" claim in the Dataset section is loose: at 47.5% the half-width is ±6.9 pp.

**W4. Evaluation subsampling is unnecessary for the free arms.** Sampling 40/assignment (200 of 1,685) is justified by LLM-arm cost, but CoEP-local, generic-only, and Refactory run on CPU at zero marginal cost; running them on all 1,685 would have cost nothing and would have tightened the paper's most load-bearing single number (47.5%, CI [40.7, 54.4] — a 14-point-wide interval). "The raw corpus supports scaling any arm" concedes the point without doing it.

**W5. Costs are modeled, not billed, and the tokenizer constant is uncalibrated.** All dollar figures derive from chars/3.6 applied to message payloads. The paper's mitigations are reasonable (uniform application, raw strings released, ±20% differential-calibration bound on break-evens), but two things weaken it: (a) if the calls were executed against real July-2026 endpoints, the API returns exact token usage per call — the stated reason for approximation ("provider tokenizers are unavailable offline") does not explain why usage metadata from the actual runs was not recorded; (b) the arms differ systematically in code/prose mix (builder output is 75k tokens of pure Python at $15/Mtok — the dominant build-cost term), and code-vs-prose chars/token ratios can differ by more than 20%, so the bound's premise deserves a calibration check on even a sample, not just an assumption. The direction of likely error (build output undercounted) inflates break-evens against the authors, but by an unquantified amount.

**W6. The emulated-endpoint protocol is underspecified.** "LLM calls ran through an agent harness constrained to a stateless single-shot endpoint (no tool use, no test execution)" leaves open: decoding parameters (temperature; single-sample outcomes at nonzero temperature add unmodeled variance to every 86.0/85.5/65.0 estimate), whether the harness injects system-prompt tokens excluded from cost accounting (which would understate all absolute m$ figures — and proportionally understate the small per-submission prompts more than the large build payloads, in the authors' favor, but this should be stated), whether fallback calls reuse the baseline arm's responses per submission (this determines whether the 0:8 discordance is structural or partly sampling noise), and exact model snapshot identifiers (the text says only "Claude Sonnet or Haiku tier, July 2026 snapshots").

**W7. Test-suite overfitting is differentially risky for the proposed method, and the RPS cap does not close the gap the paper claims it does.** The Discussion argues the cap "bounds the degenerate form of that overfit (wholesale replacement)." But the non-degenerate form — special-casing visible test inputs within a small edit — is precisely what a search that "optimizes that oracle directly" over 5-11 visible tests can do, and what a synthesized rule (whose author saw T) could even embed deliberately. One-shot per-submission generation, with no test access at generation time, is structurally *less* exposed to this. No hidden-test or mutation-based check of patch correctness is run for any arm, so the claimed quality parity is parity on an oracle the local method optimizes and the baseline does not. Auditability of the rules mitigates but does not measure this.

**W8. Single synthesis trajectory per assignment; builder variance unmeasured.** The 47.5% figure rests on one stochastic builder run per assignment (5 runs; 10 calls total), one draw of B_build (seed 7), and one draw of 3 references. The paper measures sensitivity to builder *tier* but not to builder *sampling*: a second frontier synthesis run per assignment would reveal whether 47.5% is a stable property or a draw from a wide distribution — particularly relevant given the paper's own evidence that trajectories are fragile (three of five needed rescue, top_k passed only in round 3).

**W9. Minor numeric and rhetorical blemishes (each small, collectively sloppy).** (a) The Section 2 decomposition 241+92+99 = 432, not the stated 439-token total; correspondingly "79% byte-identical" is 340/432 — against the stated 439 it is 77.4%. (b) Mean local repair time is 0.8 s in Deployment (\CoepMeanSec) but 0.9 s in Table 1 and RQ1 text. (c) "Fronting the cascade delivers 90.5% at the study's lowest marginal cost" (Intro, repeated in RQ2) is contradicted by Table 2: the Haiku row's 0.90 m$ is lower than 1.07; the claim needs a "at baseline-or-better quality" qualifier. (d) Intro says "1.9-2.5x less" where the RQ2 box says "1.8-2.5x" (different comparisons, same-sounding range). (e) numbers.tex carries stale macros (\BaseMeanRPS = 0.187 — which is actually Refactory's table value, not the baseline's 0.102; \NumBuggy = 225; \BreakEvenNCached = 257) — none referenced harmfully, but the \BaseMeanRPS mismatch invites a future wrong citation. (f) Only in the caption/text pair for Refactory do 0.187 (cap-compliant mean) and 0.386 (test-passing mean) coexist; the distinction is stated but easy to misread.

## 3. Questions for the Authors

1. **Tau sensitivity (W1).** Provide quality-repair-rate-vs-tau curves (tau in {0.2, 0.25, 0.35, 0.5, 0.75}) for the frontier baseline, retry arm, CoEP-local, and CoEP hybrids. Does "hybrid exceeds the plain baseline" survive at tau = 0.5? Does the cost-at-matched-quality claim (the paper's core) hold at all tau, as I expect it does?
2. **Overfitting audit (W7).** For each arm, what fraction of quality repairs survives held-out or mutation-generated tests (or even the other assignments' original unparsed tests, if any were dropped at import)? Separately: did you audit the 25 synthesized rule sets for test-constant embedding (grep for test literals in the repairer source)?
3. **Fallback response reuse (W6).** For a submission where CoEP-local abstains, is the hybrid's fallback outcome the *same recorded response* as the baseline arm's for that submission, or a fresh sample? Same question for the cascade and retry compositions. This determines whether discordant 0:8 / 0:0 are structural identities or include sampling luck.
4. **Decoding and harness accounting (W5, W6).** What temperature/max-token settings were used; did the agent harness prepend any system prompt, and if so, is it included in the priced payloads? Why were provider-reported usage token counts not recorded, given real endpoints were called?
5. **Tokenizer calibration (W5).** Report chars/token measured by any concrete tokenizer (e.g., an open-source BPE as proxy) separately for (i) builder outputs (pure code), (ii) per-submission prompts, (iii) per-submission outputs. What is the realized differential vs. the assumed uniform 3.6, and the recomputed break-evens?
6. **Builder variance (W8).** For at least two assignments (suggest remove_extras and search — the extremes), re-run frontier synthesis 3-5 times with different seeds: what is the spread of held-out local repair rate?
7. **Full-corpus local evaluation (W4).** Report CoEP-local, generic-only, and Refactory on all 1,685 eligible submissions. Does 47.5% hold?
8. **Arithmetic (W9a).** Reconcile 241+92+99 = 432 with the 439 average input tokens; and 0.8 vs. 0.9 s mean local time.
9. **Per-assignment traffic vs. break-even.** The RQ2 box claims break-even "inside one offering for four of five assignments" for N* in 154-295, but only min/mean/max volumes (108/337/557) are given. Please list the five per-assignment buggy-submission counts against the applicable N*.
10. **Duplicate structure.** How many of the 200 evaluation submissions are near-duplicates (e.g., token-edit distance < 0.05) of another student's submission or of B_build? This bears both on the dismissed patch-cache alternative and on the effective independent sample size.

## 4. Significance

**Score: 3/4.** The deployment-paradigm observation (amortize specification-level LLM reasoning into a verifiable per-assignment artifact) is crisp, correctly scoped, and transfers beyond APR (the closing recipe is plausible for rubrics/hints). The composition results — same quality set as any per-submission protocol at a 1.8-2.5x smaller slope — are the kind of result the cost-efficiency literature needs. What holds it below a 4: at CS1 prompt sizes the absolute stakes are tiny (the 1.30 m$/submission saving over the plain baseline is about $0.44 per assignment per term at this corpus's volume; ~$150/assignment-term even at 100k-submission MOOC scale against the retry protocol), so the practical case leans on the operational properties (local, <1 s, deterministic, auditable, no rate limits) that are asserted but not measured, and the demonstrated regime is 5 assignments in one course. The paradigm's significance grows with prompt/protocol richness (their own few-shot and retry arms at 6.53 and 3.26 m$ show the trend), which the paper argues but does not demonstrate beyond this corpus.

## 5. Justification of the Approach

**Score: 3/4.** The design is well motivated and the trust architecture is right: synthesized code cannot self-report success, every patch is independently re-executed, the size cap is enforced identically on all arms, and the hybrid structurally cannot underperform its fallback. The break-even formalism is elementary but exactly what the claim needs. Deductions: the load-bearing quality constant tau = 0.35 is inherited rather than justified or stress-tested (W1); the one-parenthesis dismissal of patch caching/deduplication is unquantified (Q10); and the framework's exposure to within-cap test special-casing (W7) is a design-level gap in the "verifiable artifact" story — verification here means "passes the visible suite," which the artifact itself optimizes.

## 6. Quality of the Evaluation

**Score: 3/4.** Substantially above the norm for this literature: same-model control, eight baselines including the strongest adversarial one (enforced-retry at 98.5%, which beats the authors' own hybrid on quality and is reported anyway), a properly re-validated classical tool with its own oracle cross-checked (91.0% vs. 90.5%), correct Wilson/McNemar statistics (I verified all of them), disclosed structural one-sidedness, and targeted ablations including a negative result. Kept from a 4 by: no tau sensitivity despite test-pass saturation making tau the sole quality discriminator (W1); the per-assignment "direction consistent" corroboration being vacuous under the superset structure (W2); n=5 clusters and free-arm subsampling (W3, W4); modeled rather than billed costs with an uncalibrated tokenizer constant (W5); no correctness check beyond the optimized visible suite (W7); single builder trajectory (W8); and the small numeric inconsistencies of W9.

## 7. Reproducibility

**Score: 3/4.** The checklist is thorough and specific: seeded scripted imports from a public corpus, deterministic local search, fixed thresholds/timeouts, per-round builder artifacts for both tiers, raw prompt/response strings released for exact re-tokenization, CPU-only execution. All analysis-level results should be exactly re-derivable from the released strings — my independent recomputation of the paper's statistics from its own reported counts succeeded everywhere, which is a good sign. Short of a 4 because: exact model snapshot IDs and decoding parameters are not in the paper ("Sonnet or Haiku tier, July 2026 snapshots"); the LLM-dependent arms are irreproducible bit-for-bit once snapshots deprecate (inherent, but exact usage metadata was apparently not captured, W5/W6); and the artifact could not be inspected for this review.

## 8. Ethical Considerations

No serious concerns. Data are from the publicly released, de-identified Refactory corpus; no new human-subjects data. The ethics statement appropriately positions the system as instructor-supervised feedback (not grading) with a no-degraded-service fallback. Two notes the authors may wish to add: (i) a synthesized repairer that silently overfits visible tests (W7) could deliver confidently wrong "feedback" to students — the instructor-audit story should be stated as a requirement, not an option; (ii) heterogeneous local coverage (25-85% by assignment) means latency/quality-of-experience differences across assignments, though the fallback equalizes outcomes.

## 9. Overall Evaluation

**Overall: 7/10 (accept).**

I went looking for the statistical or accounting hole in this paper and largely did not find one: every confidence interval, every exact p-value, every cost figure, ratio, and break-even I recomputed matches, including subtle details most papers get wrong (amortizing the cache write into the cached bound; composed fallback costs exceeding the naive rate-times-cost product because escalated subsets are harder; the interlocking 87/8/95 discordance accounting). The paper also volunteers the two facts most damaging to a naive reading — that an enforced-retry per-submission protocol reaches 98.5%, above the hybrid, and that hybrid-vs-baseline tests are structurally one-sided — and then builds its actual claim (identical quality set at 1.8x lower slope, instance-verified) on the defensible ground. The remaining weaknesses are real but bounded: the RQ1 quality-superiority headline is conditional on an untested tau = 0.35 (W1) and dressed in a vacuous per-assignment consistency check (W2); external validity rests on 5 assignments, one model family, and a 2019-public corpus with contamination handled by argument rather than by one fresh assignment; costs are modeled rather than billed; and the visible-suite oracle is one the proposed method optimizes directly (W7). None of these undermines the central, titled contribution — the cost-structure result at matched quality — which is demonstrated with unusual care within its stated scope. With the tau-sensitivity curve, an overfitting audit, and the W9 corrections, this would be a clearly strong paper; as submitted it is a good one.

**Confidence: 4/5.** I verified the statistical and cost claims independently, know the provider pricing/caching mechanics well, and am confident in the accounting analysis; I could not inspect the artifact or re-run the pipeline, and per-assignment raw counts needed for two claims are not in the paper.
